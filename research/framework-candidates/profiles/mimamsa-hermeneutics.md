# Mīmāṃsā hermeneutics candidate profile

Status: sourced-candidate / research-only / lineage-sensitive

## Identity

- names: Mīmāṃsā / Pūrvamīmāṃsā hermeneutics, ミーマーンサー解釈学
- current scope: selected structures for interpreting prescriptive Vedic texts and sentence meaning
- intended CSW use: prescriptive-unit detection, sentence/purpose unity, direct-vs-implied reading, and norm-conflict decomposition
- not intended use: treating Mīmāṃsā as a universal legal or textual interpretation algorithm

## Structural core retained at this stage

### Prescriptive-unit detection

Mīmāṃsā developed tools for deciding which words and passages form one prescription rather than several unrelated statements.

For CSW, this becomes a question of **where one actionable or normative unit begins and ends**.

### Sentence / purpose unity

A candidate sentence or passage should not be grouped merely because the words are adjacent. Unity can depend on the purpose or action conveyed.

### Linguistic connection

Bhāṭṭa discussions make distinctions such as:

- proximity (sannidhi);
- semantic fitness (yogyatā);
- syntactic expectancy (ākāṅkṣā).

These distinctions are valuable because they prevent "the terms occur near each other" from becoming the only reason to bind them.

### Direct and indirect meaning

Scriptural hermeneutics distinguishes cases where a passage is read directly from cases where secondary or contextual interpretation is invoked.

For CSW, the useful operation is to preserve the difference between:

- explicitly stated;
- required by syntax;
- supplied by context;
- inferred or implied.

### Norm-conflict decomposition

When two prescriptions appear to conflict, ask whether they concern the same action, agent, condition, purpose, or level before declaring a contradiction.

## Bhāṭṭa / Prābhākara boundary

The two major Mīmāṃsā traditions differ on sentence meaning.

Do not flatten these into one algorithm. In particular, keep open whether contextual interconnection belongs to what words themselves convey or is constructed after word meanings are first obtained.

The current profile uses only distinctions that can be stated without deciding the full dispute.

## What not to import by default

- Vedic authority as authority for unrelated target texts;
- ritual injunctions as general moral or legal commands;
- one Mīmāṃsā sub-school as the whole tradition;
- a guarantee that one interpretation is uniquely correct;
- later Vedānta use of hermeneutic devices as if it were identical to early Mīmāṃsā;
- Sanskrit linguistic assumptions as universal facts about all languages.

## Exploratory prompts

- Is this one prescription or several?
- What is the purpose that supposedly makes these clauses one unit?
- Which words actually expect one another syntactically?
- Which combinations are semantically fit, and which are merely adjacent?
- What is stated directly, and what has been supplied from context?
- Do two rules really conflict under the same agent, action, time, condition, and purpose?
- If the prescriptive frame is removed, which interpretation distinctions still survive?

## De-binding

1. remove Vedic and ritual authority claims unless historically relevant;
2. restate the issue as document-unit, purpose, syntax, semantic fit, context, implication, and conflict;
3. mark which interpretive link is explicit versus supplied;
4. keep competing segmentations when the text does not force one;
5. return conclusions to the target document and domain rules rather than the framework.
